\section{Discussion}
The results show limited agreement between LLMs and human assessors on CLAIM compliance. Agreement was low (MAE: 21.7 - 33.1 pp), and LLMs were generally stricter than humans, except in “Model performance”. This may reflect the prompt, which required explicit reporting and answering No when information was missing, insufficient, unclear or inadequate, whereas human assessors may have accepted implicit or partial information. The “Model performance” pattern, where Claude and Gemini were more lenient, is harder to explain, as the prompt also required marking inadequate information as No.

The largest discrepancy was in “Model description”, where human scores were substantially higher than those of all three LLMs, possibly because the detailed technical information required is only partially reported. Gemini’s scores were closest to human scores there, yet its correlations with humans were among the most negative, so similar score levels do not imply better agreement.

Correlations with human assessors were consistently low ($\rho$ = -0.29 to 0.02), while agreement among LLMs was higher (up to $\rho$ = 0.65). Correcting scoring bias alone would therefore not ensure agreement. This pattern could also reflect noise in human scores or biases shared among LLMs, which cannot be ruled out without human-human agreement as a reference. 

These findings support previous reports of limited agreement in manuscript evaluation \cite{saad-2024}, \cite{suleiman-2024}, but differ regarding strictness, as LLMs were generally stricter rather than more lenient. Structured criteria did not guarantee agreement, and performance varied across models, consistent with earlier model-dependent results \cite{pluimer-2026}, \cite{tsybulnik-2026}. 
\subsection{Limitations and improvements}
This study has four main limitations, each pointing to a possible improvement. First, we had no item-level human assessments, so we could not identify which CLAIM items drive the disagreements, item-level comparisons are the natural next step. Second, human scores are themselves subjective and can contain errors, so our results measure disagreement with humans, not absolute accuracy, including human-human agreement as a reference would show how much disagreement to expect even between people. Third, the sample was the first 60 available papers from a single imaging field, scored with CLAIM 2020, which limits generalization. Larger samples, other imaging domains and CLAIM 2024 should be tested. Fourth, we used one prompt and one run per paper with free model versions, so prompt sensitivity and run-to-run variability remain unassessed. Multiple prompts, repeated runs and models with extended reasoning should be evaluated, together with simple baselines and calibration or fine-tuning strategies, to establish whether LLM reliability can be improved and whether LLMs add value in CLAIM assessment.
